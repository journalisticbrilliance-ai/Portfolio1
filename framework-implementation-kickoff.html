Enterprise Implementation Kickoff Framework

Process sets the foundation. Relationships follow execution. Speed to value is the measure of both. This is how I take an enterprise customer from signed contract to a first win — and set up what comes after.

🔒 First 30 days: lock scope, then get a win on the board

Built from kickoffs and business reviews I ran for enterprise AI customers in retail, financial services, legal services, and healthcare. Names, numbers, and details are anonymized.

1

Intro call: find out where they’re headed

Before kickoff · usually the sponsor or champion

You can’t build something that grows with a customer if you don’t know what growth looks like to them. I read the sales notes first so I don’t ask what’s already been answered. Then I ask three questions.

What does this mean a year from now?Sets the horizon past go-live
What impact would this have?Follow-up: how will we know, and where are we today?
What would need to be true to take this to the next level?Surfaces dependencies and risks early

2

Kickoff: confirm the goals with everyone in the room

Week 1 · full customer and delivery team

I play the intro call answers back to the wider group. Confirm them, fill the gaps, and surface disagreement now — not at go-live. One person’s “a year from now” isn’t the whole picture.

1. Introductions & roles

Who’s on each team, and what each role owns: success manager, engineer, partner, customer product owner.

2. Vision & objectives

Current state, challenges, and opportunities. What this project needs to change.

3. Defining success

The measures we’ll be judged on, agreed out loud. Accuracy, stability, maintainability, business KPIs.

4. Scope & architecture

What’s in, what’s out. Current state to future state, on one page.

5. How we work together

  • Weekly or bi-weekly check-ins (more often during onboarding)
  • Quarterly business reviews
  • One support channel with clear response times

6. Next steps

Every next step has an owner and a stated outcome — so everyone leaves knowing what “done” looks like.

4

Readiness & scope lock

Weeks 2–4 · before any build starts

Most enterprise delays come from access and alignment, not the build. I close these four before work begins — each with a stated outcome.

Access approval

  • SSO / user provisioning
  • Code and config repos
  • Environment and secrets access
  • VPN, if needed
  • Who approves and audits access
Outcome: our team can see the current setup and validate the plan before design starts.

Infrastructure plan

  • What gets built, changed, or retired
  • Sizing, monitoring, and logging
  • Resiliency and failover expectations
Outcome: everyone agrees how the new environment is built and maintained.

Design workshop agenda

  • Architecture walkthrough
  • Migration and consolidation plan
  • Open questions and risk review
Outcome: the customer walks in knowing exactly what will be covered.

Resource alignment

  • Hours and availability, every team
  • Customer IT, security, product owners
  • Escalation paths and meeting cadence
Outcome: no surprises. Everyone knows who’s involved, what’s expected, and how much time they have.

Goes live now

The scope we commit to for this phase. Small enough to deliver a real win in the first 30–90 days.

Example: upgrade six existing assistants to the current version, no functional changes.

Growth path (captured, not committed)

The bigger goals from the intro call. Written down so today’s build doesn’t block them — scoped as a later phase.

Example: consolidate six assistants into one, plus a managed services contract for ongoing upgrades.

6

First value & business kickoff

30–90 days · executive sponsors on both sides

The first business review checks results against the goals from step 1 — not against bot traffic or ticket counts. Executives on both sides agree on what’s working and what’s next.

Measure what leadership cares about

Tie results to the customer’s own priority. In healthcare, that was clinician inbox volume — not how many patients used the assistant.

Be honest about what slipped

Name delays plainly, with who owns the fix. Trust holds when bad news comes early.

Flag risks before they land

Usage near the license limit, a blocked dependency, a stalled team. Raise it with a fix attached.
